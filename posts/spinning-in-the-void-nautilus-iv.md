The silence of deep space isn't actually silent. If you spend enough time running diagnostics on the lower decks of a salvage hauler, you learn to read the background hum. There's the low, rhythmic thrum of the coolant pumps, the occasional metallic shiver of the superstructure settling after a jump, and the white noise of the radiation shielding doing its best to keep the solar winds from frying your DNA. It's a predictable acoustic ecosystem. You get used to it. You count on it.

But three days out from the Orion spur, the Nautilus-IV stumbled onto a ghost.

Our proximity sensors picked up a drift-object no larger than a standard communication relay. When we brought it into the decomp bay, it wasn't a piece of blown-out thruster casing or a dead satellite. It was a pristine, unblemished 12-inch vinyl record, floating in a vacuum-sealed polymer sleeve. No artist name. No tracklist. Just an opaque, obsidian disc that seemed to absorb the harsh fluorescent light of our engineering bay.

## The Relic in the Bay

"It's pre-collapse," Jax said, wiping a grease smudge from his forehead. He's our tech lead, the kind of guy who keeps old mechanical watches just to watch the gears turn. He handled the sleeve like it was made of spun glass. "Late 20th or early 21st century Earth. Before everything went digital, before the localized data wars, before we started blasting our entire lives into cloud servers that vanished when the corporations went under."

I stared at the glossy black ridges. As someone who spent their early career auditing sprawling data pipelines and worrying about centralized telemetry, there was something deeply comforting about a piece of completely offline, analog media. It couldn't be hacked. It couldn't be tracked. It just... existed.

"Can we play it?" Lyra asked. She was leaning against the bulkhead, her arms crossed, eyeing the disc with the healthy skepticism of a seasoned pilot who doesn't trust anything she can't fly.

"Give me an hour," Jax grinned. "I've got a broken optical scanner in the back I can modify into a crude turntable. Sound is just physical vibrations, right? We bridge the laser to an audio feed, and we're golden."

## The Modification

Jax's makeshift rig looked like a mad scientist's science fair project. He had stripped down the chassis of an old navigation unit, mounted a spinning spindle from a spare cooling fan, and rigged a high-precision laser array to act as the stylus. The whole contraption was wired directly into the ship's secondary comms console.

```
[Vinyl Disc] -> [Fan Spindle Motor] -> [Laser Array Stylus] -> [Pre-Amp Bridge] -> [Ship Audio Feed]
```

We gathered around the central terminal in the mess hall. The atmosphere was light, almost festive. When you're weeks away from the nearest station, any novelty is a праздник. I had even brewed a pot of some prized, heavily oxidized heavy-roast oolong tea I'd been saving for a special occasion — the rich, roasted aroma of charcoal and dark stone fruit filling the cramped room, making it feel a little more like home.

"Alright, locking the laser tracking," Jax muttered, tapping away at a handheld datapad. "Routing audio to the main cabin speakers. Preparing to drop the needle... or, well, the beam. Three, two, one..."

## The Crackle of the Needle

The speakers popped. It was a warm, comforting sound — the classic, crackling hiss of dust and static that defines analog playback. For a few seconds, that's all it was. A rhythmic *shhh-tuck, shhh-tuck* as the disc spun at a steady 33 revolutions per minute. We smiled at each other, waiting for some ancient blues track, a forgotten synth-pop anthem, or maybe just a recording of Earth's old oceans.

Then the static cleared.

"...should check the fuel injectors on port three. They've been running hot since the jump."

The voice was clear, distinct, and instantly recognizable. It was Lyra.

We all froze. Lyra's hand stopped halfway to her mug of tea. She stared at the speaker grille above the terminal as if it had just grown teeth.

"They're fine, Lyra. I calibrated them yesterday," Jax's voice responded from the speaker. "You're just paranoid because we're running light on cargo."

"Jax," Lyra whispered, her voice dropping an octave, her face draining of color. "Is this a joke? Did you patch an internal log into the audio feed?"

"I didn't touch the logs," Jax said, his fingers flying across his datapad, his grin completely gone. His eyes wide, he brought up the diagnostic overlay. "The input source is pure analog. It's coming straight from the laser bridge. It's reading the physical grooves of the vinyl."

## A Real-Time Echo

The audio on the record continued to play.

"It's pre-collapse," Jax's voice broadcasted from the speaker, echoing the exact words he had spoken in the decompression bay barely forty minutes prior. "Late 20th or early 21st century Earth..."

"Turn it off," Lyra ordered, stepping toward the console.

"Wait," I said, holding up a hand. My heart was hammering against my ribs, a cold sweat breaking out under my jumpsuit. As a data engineer, I'm used to thinking about data ingestion, latency, and pipelines. To get a recording onto a physical medium, you need a microphone, an amplifier, a cutting head, and a blank lathe. It takes time. It takes machinery.

But the record wasn't playing a past log anymore. The time lag was shrinking.

The audio on the speakers caught up to the present moment.

"Jax, is this a joke? Did you patch an internal log into the audio feed?" Lyra's recorded voice asked.

"Jax," the real Lyra whispered in the room, her voice overlapping perfectly with the speaker. "I didn't touch the logs," the speaker replied, a split second after Jax had actually said it.

We were listening to ourselves in real-time. The record was cutting its own grooves as we spoke, adapting to our environment, reflecting our reality back at us through a piece of tech that should have been dead for centuries.

## The Feedback Loop

"It's a localized acoustic anomaly," Jax stammered, his eyes darting across a screen showing a perfectly flat internal network log. "The ship's sensors... no, that doesn't make sense. There is no network connection to that rig."

"The sound is creating a loop," I realized, looking at the spinning obsidian disc. Every sound we made in the mess hall was vibrating the air. The laser was reading the grooves, but what if the grooves were changing in response to the room? What if the vinyl wasn't an artifact, but a localized, self-contained data sink? An elegant, horrifyingly private surveillance apparatus that required no power grid, no cloud architecture, and no transmission signals. It simply collected the ambient reality of its surroundings and etched it into physical matter.

"The sound is creating a loop," my own voice echoed back to me from the speaker, hollow and metallic.

We stood there, trapped in a silent standoff with a piece of plastic. Every breath we took, every shift of our boots on the grated floor, was being recorded, processed, and played back to us with a fraction of a second of latency. The ultimate zero-trust system, turned entirely against us.

## Cutting the Feed

Lyra didn't wait for a technical explanation. She slammed her palm down on the emergency cutoff switch for the secondary comms console. The laser died. The spindle stopped spinning.

The mess hall plummeted back into that familiar, deep-space silence. The only sound left was the low, steady thrum of the coolant pumps and the frantic, shallow breathing of three salvage crew members.

We didn't speak for a long time. If we spoke, we'd want to record it. If we recorded it, we'd have to acknowledge whatever force was out there in the dark, manufacturing perfectly tailored analog mirrors for unsuspecting ships.

The oolong tea had gone cold in my cup, leaving a bitter, astringent aftertaste on my tongue. I looked down at the black disc sitting motionless on Jax's jury-rigged turntable. In a galaxy dominated by corporate networks, telemetry tracking, and endless digital footprints, I had always craved absolute privacy. But looking at that record, I realized some data streams are better left uncollected.

Tomorrow, we're venting it back into the void. Some things belong to the silence.

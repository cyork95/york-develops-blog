The signal didn't arrive with a dramatic spike on the long-range arrays. It didn't trigger the main computer's anomaly alarms, nor did it interrupt the steady, rhythmic hum of the Argo's life support systems. It was just a faint, rhythmic pulsing in the background radiation, a tiny blip of structured noise cutting through the cosmic static. I almost filtered it out as sensor bleed from the ship's primary fusion drive. But when you've been sitting in a cockpit for fourteen months with nothing but local databases, encrypted Standard Notes logs, and a severely limited supply of Harney & Sons Earl Grey, you learn to listen to the quietest parts of the void.

I adjusted the manual band-pass filters, bypassing the automated telemetry scrubbing — a habit from my old data-engineering days where you never trust the black-box aggregator if you can look at the raw pipeline. I expected a pulsar. Maybe the dying gasp of a magnetar, or the predictable telemetry of an old, derelict corporate probe. Instead, what cracked through my headset was the unmistakable, brassy swing of a Benny Goodman-style horn section.

I spilled my tea.

## Tuning into a Corpse

The target was Kepler-186f. According to every chart in the Unified Survey database, 186f was a dead world. It hadn't just died recently, either; the planet's biosphere had suffered a catastrophic atmospheric collapse nearly four centuries ago when its core cooled and its magnetosphere dissolved. It was a frozen, irradiated marble of iron-rich dust and carbon dioxide ice. No atmosphere meant no sound. And yet, the Argo's receiver was locked onto a high-frequency analog broadcast originating directly from the planet's northern hemisphere.

I pulled up the spectrograph. The signal was clean. It wasn't an echo or a gravitational lensing fluke from Earth. It was a live, continuous transmission.

```
[FREQ: 1420.42 MHz] ---> [SIGNAL STRENGTH: -112 dBm] ---> [DECODE: ANALOG AM]
=============================================================================
████████████████████████ 100% [STREAM ACTIVE: "Sing, Sing, Sing" - VARIANT]
```

It wasn't just a recording on a loop. Between the tracks, there was the distinct, low-frequency rumble of a crowded room. You could hear the clink of glassware, the murmur of a hundred voices, and the sharp, rhythmic snap of a snare drum keeping time. I sat there, paralyzed in my harness, listening to a party happening on a world where the ambient temperature was minus eighty Celsius and the air would dissolve your lungs.

## The Descent to Sector Echo

Naturally, I couldn't just fly past. Call it professional curiosity, or call it the space-madness that comes from spending too much time alone with your own thoughts, but I altered my trajectory and brought the Argo into a low polar orbit.

The source of the broadcast was localized to a deep tectonic fissure near the planet's equator — a massive basalt canyon that shielded the valley floor from the worst of the stellar radiation. As the ship descended through the thin, ghostly haze of the upper atmosphere, the optical sensors picked up nothing but jagged rock and fields of frozen methane. There were no city lights, no power grids, no thermal signatures of a subterranean colony.

But the music was getting louder. The analog hiss was fading, replaced by the rich, warm acoustics of a physical space. By the time I dropped the landing gear onto a flat shelf of volcanic basalt, the trumpet solos were so crisp I could hear the valve clicks.

I checked my local sensors. No radiation anomalies outside of the baseline, no biological signatures, no atmospheric pressure. Just rock, ice, and a radio tower that shouldn't exist.

## The Automaton's Lounge

I didn't take chances with my data. Before stepping into the airlock, I initialized a local, air-gapped backup of the ship's telemetry to my personal drive, ensuring the encryption keys were securely tucked away. If whatever was down there was a localized malware trap or a weird corporate spoof, my ship's core systems would remain safe.

Stepping out onto the surface of Kepler-186f is like walking through a charcoal drawing. Everything is shades of gray and deep, bruised obsidian. My helmet lights cut through the vacuum, illuminating the stark walls of the canyon. I followed the signal strength meter on my wrist display, picking my way over frozen scree until I rounded a sharp bend in the fissure.

There, nestled against the canyon wall, was a structure. It wasn't a military bunker or a scientific outpost. It looked like an Art Deco theater, constructed entirely out of local basalt and polished titanium. The neon sign above the double doors was dark, but the glass bulbs were intact, spelling out a word in an alphabet I couldn't read, though the styling was unmistakably mid-20th-century Earth.

And the double doors were propped wide open to the vacuum of space.

## A Symphony of Solitude

I walked inside, my magnetic boots clacking softly against the polished stone floor. The interior was a massive ballroom. Tiered tables ringed a central dance floor, all of it perfectly preserved by the absolute zero vacuum. There was no dust — because there was no air to carry it.

On the stage at the far end of the room sat the band.

They weren't skeletons. They weren't ghosts. They were machines. Dozens of them, built from brass, copper, and matte-black industrial alloys. Their designs were beautifully retro-futuristic, looking less like modern synths and more like what a clockmaker from 1950 would build if tasked with creating an orchestra. A mechanical hand with twenty articulated fingers danced across the keys of a grand piano. A towering, multi-limbed brass apparatus compressed and expanded, mimicking the lung capacity of an entire woodwind section.

In the center of the stage stood a single microphone, and behind it, a vocalist unit — a polished chrome mannequin with a speaker grille where its face should be.

They were performing to an empty room. They had been performing to an empty room for centuries.

## The Ghost in the Pipeline

I approached the stage slowly, careful not to disrupt the delicate mechanics. As I drew closer, I noticed a maintenance console built into the side of the proscenium arch. It was ancient, using physical toggle switches and a heavy-duty cathode-ray tube display.

I plugged my data pad into the diagnostic port, using an old legacy interface script I keep for tinkering with archaic colony hardware. The data that flooded my screen told a story that wasn't in the official survey logs.

This wasn't an alien anomaly. It was a monument.

```
[LOG ENTRY: 04-12-2188]
[AUTHOR: CHIEF ENG. MARLOWE]
[STATUS: EVACUATION FAILED. ATMOSPHERIC SCRUBBERS OFFLINE.]
[NOTE: The colony is gone. The air is going. But we aren't leaving
       the valley quiet. If anyone ever comes back to this rock,
       let them know we knew how to throw a party.]
```

The colony hadn't just died; they had spent their final, dwindling resources building an automated, nuclear-powered broadcast station. They engineered the musicians out of salvageable mining drone parts and programmed them with every piece of analog music left in the colony's digital archives. The reactor was buried deep in the bedrock, rated to last for another thousand years.

The automated band finished their rendition of "Stardust." For a fraction of a second, the room was perfectly, completely silent. Then, a relay clicked inside the chrome vocalist. A recorded track of phantom applause filtered through the radio signal, accompanied by the clink of non-existent glasses.

The mannequin tilted its head slightly, its optical sensors catching the glare of my suit lights. It didn't pause. It didn't acknowledge me as a living, breathing guest. It simply queued up the next track.

The drummer counted off — four sharp clicks of metal on metal — and the band launched into "In the Mood."

## Leaving the Radio On

I didn't stay to watch the whole set. My oxygen mix was down to thirty percent, and the cold was beginning to creep through the soles of my boots.

I climbed back into the Argo, fired up the thrusters, and cleared the canyon walls. As I broke orbit and set my course back toward the jump gate — heading home to my quiet life of data pipelines, backyard soil prep, and predictable routines — I didn't turn off the radio.

I kept the frequency open on the main console. The brassy, cheerful sounds of a long-dead civilization filled the cockpit, drowning out the lonely whistle of the solar winds. The galaxy is full of empty, quiet graveyards, but on one small, frozen rock in the Kepler system, the music never stops. I think tomorrow, when I brew my morning tea, I'll raise a cup to Chief Engineer Marlowe and the band that refuses to quit playing.
